We are named after one of these. El lazo is the cord looped in a figure eight around a couple after their vows, and it is the reason this company is called what it is — so writing carelessly about anyone else's ceremony was never going to be an option.

Most wedding sites give a tradition a paragraph inside a listicle, usually written by someone who has never seen it happen. These pages describe what actually occurs, who does it, where practice varies by country and family, and the concrete thing you have to tell a vendor for it to go well. Where a tradition carries a history that matters, we say so rather than reducing it to decor.

Including a tradition that isn't yours

Couples marry across cultures constantly, and blending two sets of rituals is one of the genuinely good problems in wedding planning. A few things make it go well.

  • Ask the family who holds it. Not the internet, and not us. The version your partner's grandmother knows is the one that will mean something in the room.
  • Have someone explain it aloud. Half your guests will not know what they are watching. Thirty seconds from the officiant turns a nice moment into the one people talk about afterwards.
  • Know which ones carry weight. Some traditions are festive and travel easily. Others — jumping the broom is the clearest example — are tied to a specific history, and taking them on without that history is not the same act.
  • Do fewer, properly. Three rituals given room beat eight crammed into a forty-minute ceremony. Every one of them needs a cue, a rehearsal and a photographer who knows it is coming.
