The tradition behind our name

What is the lazo?

The wedding lasso ceremony: a single cord, looped in a figure eight, that makes a bond visible.

All wedding traditions

Midway through many Mexican, Filipino, and Spanish wedding ceremonies, after the vows have been spoken, something quietly beautiful happens. Family members — often the couple's padrinos, their chosen godparents for the marriage — step forward carrying a single long cord. Sometimes it's a strand of flowers, sometimes a beaded rope, often an oversized rosary. They loop it gently around the shoulders of the couple in the shape of a figure eight: one loop for each person, joined at the centre.

That cord is el lazo — the lasso — and the figure eight is no accident. Turned on its side, it's the symbol of infinity. Two separate lives, bound into one unending shape. The couple wears it through the rest of the ceremony, through the prayers and the blessing, physically tied together as they hear the words that make them family. When it's lifted away, it's kept — draped over a mirror, framed on a wall, passed down — a keepsake of the exact moment two people became one.

The lazo doesn't create the bond. It makes the bond visible.

Where the tradition comes from

The lazo ceremony has roots in Spanish Catholic practice and grew into one of the most beloved traditions of Mexican and Filipino weddings, alongside las arras (the thirteen coins) and the veil ceremony. Together these are often called the three ceremonial gifts, and they tend to appear in the same part of the service: the veil for protection, the coins for provision, the lazo for unity.

Today couples of every background borrow it, because what it says needs no translation: marriage is a binding. Chosen, celebrated, witnessed, and meant to hold.

Who places the lazo

The padrinos de lazo — the godparents of the lasso — are a married couple the bride and groom choose: often parents, grandparents, or the couple whose marriage they'd most like theirs to resemble. They place the cord and, traditionally, they stand behind the marriage for life — the people you call when it's hard. Being asked is an honour, and it is usually asked in person.

When it happens, and what to expect

The lazo comes after the vows and rings, generally just before the final blessing. The couple kneels or stands close while the padrinos settle the cord over both sets of shoulders. It stays on through the remaining prayers — a few minutes, sometimes longer — and is lifted away at the end of the ceremony, at which point it belongs to the couple.

If you're planning one: tell your officiant early so the order of service makes room for it, and tell your photographer earlier still. The placing and the lifting are two of the most photographed seconds of the whole day, and they happen once, from one angle, with no second take. It's the kind of moment worth choosing a photographer who has shot it before.

Choosing a lazo

There is no single correct object. A double rosary is the most traditional; a satin or silk cord is the most common; flowers woven the length of the loop are the most photographed. Families who have kept one for generations use that one, and a cord that has been on three marriages before yours carries a weight no florist can supply. If you're starting fresh, many couples ask a grandmother to make it, which is its own kind of beginning.
