How to get your full deposit back.

Deposit deductions are where good tenancies go to die. Most of them are preventable — if you start on move-in day, not move-out day.

By the LeaseReputation team · Updated 2026-09-28

The game is won on move-in day

Deposit disputes come down to one question: was the damage there before you moved in? If you can't prove it was, the property's version wins by default. So before a single box crosses the threshold:

  • Photograph everything. Every wall, every corner of carpet, inside the oven, under the sinks, every blind and screen. Wide shots for context, close-ups for existing damage. Video works too — narrate the date as you walk.
  • Fill out the move-in condition report completely. If the property gives you a checklist, treat it like a legal document — because it is. "Everything fine" is what they hope you write. Write the truth: the scuff behind the door, the chipped tile, the stain the size of a coffee mug.
  • Send it in writing. Email the completed report and a link to your photos to the leasing office so there's a timestamp neither side can dispute.

Know what they're allowed to charge you for

Landlords can deduct for damage, not for normal wear and tear — and the difference is defined by law in most states, not by the property's mood. Faded paint, minor scuffs, worn carpet in walkways, small nail holes from hanging pictures: that's wear, and in most states it can't come out of your deposit. Broken doors, pet stains, holes in drywall, burns: that's damage.

Watch for "cleaning fee" games. Some properties deduct a flat cleaning fee from everyone regardless of condition. In many states that's only enforceable if it's explicitly in your lease — and sometimes not even then. Read the deposit section of your lease before you sign, not after you move out.

Move-out: run the same play in reverse

  • Clean like it matters, because it does. Ovens, baseboards, inside the fridge. Cleaning charges are the most common deduction and the easiest to prevent.
  • Patch small nail holes with spackle — five dollars of supplies against a "wall repair" line item.
  • Photograph everything again, same coverage as move-in. Same-day timestamps.
  • Request a walkthrough with staff and get any "looks good" in writing. Several states give you the legal right to a pre-move-out inspection — use it.
  • Give your forwarding address in writing. In most states the deadline clock for returning your deposit doesn't start until they have it.

If they keep it anyway

Every state sets a deadline (commonly 14–30 days) for the landlord to return your deposit or send an itemized list of deductions. Miss the deadline or skip the itemization, and in many states they forfeit the right to keep anything — some states award you two or three times the deposit as a penalty.

  1. Send a demand letter. Certified mail. State the amount, cite your state's deposit statute, attach your move-in and move-out photos, give them 10 days.
  2. Small claims court is built for exactly this. Filing costs $30–75, you don't need a lawyer, and a tenant with dated photos and a paper trail wins far more often than not.
  3. Tell the next renter. A property that plays deposit games with you is playing them with everyone. A verified review — with your deposit outcome on the record — is how the pattern becomes visible.
