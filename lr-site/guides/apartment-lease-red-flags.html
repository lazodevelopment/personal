9 lease clauses that should stop your pen.

Nobody reads the lease — which is exactly what the worst clauses count on. These nine are worth the twenty minutes.

By the LeaseReputation team · Updated 2026-09-28

1. Fee stacking buried past page one

The rent on the listing and the monthly total in the lease are often different documents' worth apart. Scan for: valet trash, amenity fees, package fees, pest control fees, mandatory insurance programs, "utility administration" fees. Add every recurring line to get your real rent — then judge the value.

2. Automatic renewal with a long notice window

Some leases quietly convert to another full term unless you give notice 60–90 days out. Miss the window and you owe another year — or a steep month-to-month premium. Calendar the notice date the day you sign.

3. Vague "excessive cleaning" deposit language

Deposit deduction clauses that don't define standards ("unit must be returned in satisfactory condition") are blank checks. Look for leases that reference normal wear and tear explicitly — and read our deposit guide before move-in day either way.

4. Unlimited entry rights

Most states require notice (commonly 24–48 hours) before non-emergency entry. A lease claiming the right to enter "at any time for any purpose" is either unenforceable, a sign of how management operates, or both.

5. The rent-increase-anytime clause

Fixed-term leases should fix the rent. Month-to-month arrangements and some sneaky fixed leases allow mid-term increases with 30 days' notice. Know which document you're holding.

6. Mandatory arbitration and class-action waivers

Increasingly common: a clause waiving your right to sue or join class actions, routing everything to an arbitrator. Courts treat these differently by state; at minimum, know you're signing one — it changes your leverage in every future dispute.

7. Repairs shifted onto you

Clauses making the tenant responsible for appliance repair, drain clearing, or "first $100 of every request" convert maintenance from their obligation into your bill, one service call at a time.

8. Early-termination terms that don't exist

A lease that's silent on early termination doesn't mean freedom — it usually means you owe every remaining month. The best leases name a concrete buyout (commonly two months' rent). No clause at all is the red flag.

9. The "as-is" unit acceptance

Language stating you accept the unit "as-is, in good condition" — signed before you've spent a night there — undercuts every future claim about pre-existing problems. Pair your signature with a written condition report and dated photos, same day.

The meta-flag: how the office reacts to questions about the lease. A property that bristles at "what does this clause mean?" is showing you exactly how disputes will go later.
