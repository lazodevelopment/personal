How to spot fake apartment reviews — and buried real ones.

Apartment review fraud runs in both directions: manufactured praise and vanished complaints. Here's how to read through both.

By the LeaseReputation team · Updated 2026-07-18

The economics make fraud inevitable

A community's review score directly moves lease-up speed, which directly moves revenue. Where a number is worth that much, an industry grows around manipulating it: reputation-management firms, review-generation campaigns, takedown services. In 2024 the FTC formally banned buying fake reviews, suppressing honest ones, and review-gating (only inviting happy residents to post) — but a rule on paper doesn't un-bury a review. The manipulation just got quieter.

The tells of a manufactured 5-star

  • Clusters. Eight glowing reviews in the same two weeks — especially around lease-up season or right after a bad press cycle — is a campaign, not a coincidence.
  • Marketing vocabulary. Real residents say "maintenance actually shows up." Campaigns say "luxurious resort-style amenities and a seamless leasing experience."
  • Reviewer history. An account whose only review ever is this apartment community, posted the week it opened, is not your neighbor.
  • Specificity asymmetry. Real praise names things: a staff member, an incident, a timeframe. Fake praise is adjectives all the way down.
  • The staff-name pattern. A suspicious run of reviews all praising the same leasing agent by name often traces to an internal contest — reviews solicited by staff, for staff.

How real reviews disappear

  • Platform disputes. On most sites, a business can flag reviews as "fake" or "policy-violating" — and an angry-but-honest review reads exactly like a fake one to an overworked moderator.
  • Review gating. Resident portals that ask "How was your experience?" and only route the happy answers to public review sites. Banned in 2024; still everywhere.
  • Settle-and-delete. "We'll refund your deposit if you take the review down." The review economy's quietest transaction.

Reading any review site defensively

  • Read the 2- and 3-stars first. Fraud concentrates at 1 and 5; the middle is where the honest texture lives.
  • Sort by recent. Management companies change; a 4.5 built in 2021 says nothing about the team running the building today.
  • Look for patterns, not incidents. One angry deposit story is a data point. Five deposit stories across two years is the business model.
  • Check whether the platform sells services to the properties it rates. If the review site's revenue comes from the buildings being reviewed, you know whose interests win the close calls.
